Lesson 1 · 30 minutes

Choose the right knife and cut for a job

By the end of this lesson you can look at a job in your kitchen, such as slicing a tomato for a sandwich or dicing an onion for soup, and name the knife and the cut that suit it.

Objective O2: choose the knife (chef's, paring, serrated) and the cut (slice, dice, julienne) About 30 minutes
Why this matters for you

You want to cook at home without cutting yourself. A knife that is wrong for the job makes you push, saw and slip, and slipping is how fingers get cut. A cut that is wrong for the dish also cooks unevenly. Choosing well is a safety skill as much as a cooking skill.

You already hold the knife and the food the safe way, with the pinch grip and the claw grip. Now you decide which knife to hold and what shape to cut.

Warm-up 2 min

Last session you learned how to hold the knife and the food. Before we add anything new, pull that back from memory. Trying to remember, even if you get it a little wrong, is what makes it stick.

Recall

Without looking back: what do your fingertips do in the claw grip, and which part of your hand does the side of the blade rest against?

Check your answer

The fingertips curl back, away from the blade, and the knuckles guide the side of the blade. Every cut in this lesson uses that same grip. Choosing a different knife or cut changes what you cut, never how your hands stay safe.

Three knives, three jobs 7 min

Open your knife drawer. You probably have a big knife, a small knife, and maybe one with a toothed edge for bread. Those three are enough for nearly everything a home cook does, and each has its shape for a reason.

A chef's knife is the big, all-purpose knife with a broad blade that curves up toward the tip. Cooking schools call it the workhorse of the kitchen: it chops, slices, dices and minces (Escoffier). A typical blade is about 8 to 10 inches long. The length and weight do the work for you on firm vegetables such as onions, carrots and potatoes, so you need little force. The broad, flat side also gives your knuckles a surface to guide against.

A paring knife is the small knife, with a blade of roughly 2 to 4 inches. It is for small, close work that a big blade is clumsy at: peeling, trimming, coring a tomato, hulling a strawberry, taking the stem and seeds out of a pepper (Escoffier).

A serrated knife has teeth along its edge, like a tiny saw. It is for food with a tough or slippery outside and a soft inside: bread above all, and also tomatoes. The teeth bite through the skin or crust, so you do not have to press down on the soft middle and squash it (Escoffier).

Cooking-school lists include many more knives: boning, slicing, cleaver and so on. You do not need them yet. These three cover most home jobs.

Two questions that pick the knife

When you are unsure which knife to take, ask two questions about the food.

First: how big and how firm is it? Big and firm, such as an onion, a carrot or a squash, calls for the chef's knife. Small or fiddly, such as a strawberry's green top or a pepper's core, calls for the paring knife.

Second: is the outside tougher or slipperier than the inside? If so, as with bread or a ripe tomato, reach for the serrated knife.

Here is a worked example. You want to slice a loaf of bread. It is big, but not firm all the way through: the crust is hard and the crumb is soft. Question two applies, so you take the serrated knife and use a gentle back-and-forth motion, letting the teeth do the work instead of pushing down.

Check yourself

You want to cut a ripe tomato into thin slices for a sandwich. The skin is slippery and the inside is soft. Which knife do you take?

Show the answer

The serrated knife. The teeth grip and break the skin, then the blade moves through the soft middle without squashing it.

Slice, julienne and dice 7 min

The knife is how you cut. The cut is the shape you cut the food into. Recipes name cuts so that all the pieces are the same size, because pieces of the same size cook in the same time. If a recipe says "dice the onion" and you chop it into random chunks, some bits will burn while others stay raw.

Here are the three cuts in this lesson. The sizes are cooking-school standards. Books differ a little, so treat them as targets and not as laws (Wikipedia: list of culinary knife cuts; Rouxbe).

Slice

A slice is a flat piece cut across the food: a slice of bread, a round of cucumber, a wheel of tomato. It is the simplest cut and the starting point for the other two. The one thing to decide is thickness: thin for a sandwich, thicker for roasting.

Julienne

Julienne (say "ZHOO-lee-en") means thin, matchstick-shaped strips. The usual cooking-school size is about 1/8 inch (3 mm) thick and wide, and about 2 to 2½ inches (5 to 6 cm) long. Think of the carrot sticks in a stir-fry or a slaw. They are thin so that they cook in a minute or two, or can be eaten raw.

A slightly thicker stick, about 1/4 inch (6 mm) square, is called a batonnet ("bah-toh-NAY"). Think of a French fry. Batonnet is the step between a slice and a dice, which you will see in a moment.

Dice

To dice is to cut food into small cubes. Dice comes in sizes. A small dice is 1/4 inch (about 6 mm) on every side. A medium dice is about 1/2 inch (12 mm), and a large dice is about 3/4 inch (about 2 cm). Cubes of 1/8 inch are called brunoise, but you can leave those for later.

Why bother with exact sizes? Evenly sized pieces finish cooking together. That is the whole point of naming a cut.
Check yourself

A soup recipe says "small dice the carrots." What shape and size are you aiming for?

Show the answer

Cubes of about 1/4 inch on each side. Julienne is matchsticks, and a slice is a flat round.

Matching the job to the knife and the cut 5 min

Now put the two halves together. For any job, answer three questions in this order.

  1. What shape does the dish want? Flat pieces, thin sticks, or cubes? This picks the cut.
  2. How big and how firm is the food? This picks between the chef's knife and the paring knife.
  3. Is the outside tougher or slipperier than the inside? If yes, the serrated knife.

Here are everyday jobs, answered.

JobKnifeCutWhy
Onion for soupChef'sDiceFirm and large; even cubes cook evenly in a pot.
Carrot for a stir-fryChef'sJulienneThin sticks cook in a minute or two.
Tomato for a sandwichSerratedSliceSlippery skin, soft inside.
Loaf of breadSerratedSliceHard crust, soft crumb.
Core a pepper, hull strawberriesParingTrimming, no named cutSmall, close work done in the hand.
Potatoes for roastingChef'sLarge diceBig cubes brown outside and stay soft inside.

Notice that a paring job sometimes has no cut name. Peeling and trimming prepare the food; they do not shape it.

Try the questions on a new case. A recipe asks for thin cucumber rounds on a salad. Shape: flat pieces, so a slice. Size and firmness: a cucumber is a medium, fairly firm vegetable with a thin skin, so the chef's knife works well. Tougher outside? Not enough to need teeth. Chef's knife, slice.

Check yourself

You are making a quick slaw and want long, thin strips of a large, firm carrot. Which pair fits best?

Show the answer

Chef's knife and julienne: large, firm food, and thin sticks.

Common mix-ups 3 min

Mix-up: "the small knife is the safe knife"

It is tempting because a small blade looks less scary. But with a paring knife you often hold the food in your hand and cut near it, which puts your fingers close to the edge. For a firm vegetable on a board, the long chef's knife with the claw grip keeps your fingertips out of the way. Choose the knife for the job, not for how it looks.

Mix-up: "the serrated knife cuts everything"

The teeth look like they would help on anything. On a firm vegetable they tend to tear and leave a ragged cut, and ragged cuts make uneven pieces. Keep the serrated knife for bread, tomatoes and other foods with a tough outside and a soft inside.

Mix-up: "dice and chop are the same, so size does not matter"

To chop is to cut roughly. To dice is to cut cubes of a named size. When a recipe says "dice", it wants even cubes so that the pieces cook at the same speed. A rough chop is fine for a rustic stew, but not when the recipe names the cut.

What about the idea that a dull knife is safer? That is for the next lesson, on keeping a knife sharp. For now, hold on to one thing: a knife that needs a hard push is one that is likely to slip.

Worked example and practice 5 min

Worked example

Job: a stir-fry needs a carrot cut into julienne. Which knife, which cut, and how?

  1. Pick the cut. A stir-fry cooks in minutes, so the pieces must be thin. Thin sticks are julienne.
  2. Pick the knife. A carrot is long and firm, so the chef's knife.
  3. Make a flat face. Trim the ends, cut a thin slice off one side, and lay the carrot on that flat side so it cannot roll. A rolling carrot is a slipping carrot.
  4. Slice into planks. Cut lengthwise slices about 1/8 inch thick, in lengths of about 2 to 2½ inches. Keep the claw grip, with knuckles guiding the blade.
  5. Cut the sticks. Stack a few planks and cut them lengthwise into strips about 1/8 inch wide. That is julienne.
  6. Check. The sticks should look like matchsticks of about the same size. Recut any odd ones, because uneven sticks cook unevenly.
Your turn

Job: a potato needs to be cut into small dice for a quick hash. The first step is given: "Pick the knife: the potato is large and firm, so the chef's knife." Now work out the rest. How do you keep the potato from rolling, and how do you get from the whole potato to cubes? Write your steps before you open the answer.

Show a solution

Cut a thin slice off one side to make a flat face, and lay the potato on it. Cut planks about 1/4 inch thick. Stack a few planks and cut them lengthwise into sticks 1/4 inch wide (batonnet). Line the sticks up and cut across every 1/4 inch to get small dice. Check that the cubes are about the same size, and recut any big ones.

Plan it yourself

Last one, with no steps given. Your dinner tonight is a salad with sliced tomato, strips of pepper, and a few strawberries with their green tops removed. Name the knife you would use for each job. Say what cut, if any, each one gets.

Show a solution

Tomato: serrated knife, slice. Pepper strips: chef's knife on a board, cut into strips (a julienne or thicker batonnet, as you like). Strawberries: paring knife to remove the green top, which is trimming and has no named cut. If you chose differently, say why; the reasoning matters more than a single right pairing.

Summary 1 min

  • Chef's knife for big, firm food; paring knife for small, close work; serrated knife for a tough outside and a soft inside.
  • A slice is a flat piece; julienne is thin matchsticks (about 1/8 inch); dice is cubes (small dice is about 1/4 inch).
  • Pick the cut from the dish, then the knife from the food.
  • Pieces of the same size cook at the same speed.

Say it in your own words: without looking up, explain which knife and which cut you would use for an onion going into soup, and why.
