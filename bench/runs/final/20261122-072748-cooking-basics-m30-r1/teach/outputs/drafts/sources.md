## 1. PRIMARY: Escoffier, "Different Knives and the Best Uses for Each One"
Address: https://escoffier.edu/blog/culinary-arts/different-knives-and-the-best-uses-for-each (opened, no redirect reported)
Cite: YES. A culinary-school blog; describe it as that.

- Chef's knife 8 to 10 in, 6 to 14 range: SUPPORTED. "typically between eight and ten inches, although it can be as short as six inches and as long as 14 inches."
- Chef's knife uses: PARTLY. Page lists "Rock chopping, slicing, dicing, julienning, chiffonade." The plan's list (onions, carrots, herbs, melons, poultry, cabbage) was not confirmed in the page text I got; cite only the verbs, or leave the foods as the lesson's own examples.
- Weight and balance: PARTLY. Page says "The more comfortable you feel with the weight and balance", tied to cutting faster. "Smooth pace" is the lesson's wording.
- Paring knife up to 3.5 in: SUPPORTED. "The blade will be no longer than 3.5 inches."
- Paring uses: SUPPORTED for apples, potatoes, garlic, onion (mincing small amounts), coring tomatoes: "great for peeling apples and potatoes, mincing small amounts of garlic and onions, and coring tomatoes". NOT FOUND for ginger or strawberries as paring jobs (strawberry appears only under serrated).
- Not for hard vegetables: SUPPORTED. "isn't suited for harder vegetables – such as carrots, beets, and squash."
- Never force a paring knife: SUPPORTED. "you never want to apply force with a paring knife." The "switch to a heavier blade" part was not confirmed; word it as the lesson's advice.
- Serrated for bread, tomato, orange, strawberry: SUPPORTED. "cut through the surface of a tomato, orange, or strawberry"; bread "without ruining the crumb structure".
- Sawing motion: SUPPORTED. "By using a gentle sawing motion."
- Cut not as clean as chef's knife: SUPPORTED. "the cut won't necessarily look as clean as the one you'll get with a chef's knife."
- Serrated not honed on a rod: SUPPORTED. Honing rod "will work with all your knives – except your serrated knives."
- Dull blades raise injury risk: SUPPORTED. "dull blades ... can also increase the risk of injury."
- NOT in this page (lesson's own advice): "dull knife squashes a tomato", "tough or slippery outside, soft inside" rule of thumb, "wide blade guides the knuckles", "a 3-inch blade must be pushed through an onion", all numbers about cook times and onion width.

## 2. Rouxbe, "Types of Knife Cuts"
Address: https://rouxbe.com/tips-techniques/495-types-of-knife-cuts (opened)
Cite: YES (cooking-school site; describe it as that).

- Large dice 3/4 in: SUPPORTED. "Large Dice – 3/4" x 3/4" x 3/4""
- Medium dice 1/2 in: SUPPORTED. "Medium Dice – 1/2" x 1/2" x 1/2""
- Small dice 1/4 in: SUPPORTED.
- Brunoise 1/8 in: SUPPORTED. Note the page lists brunoise as its own cut, not as a dice size; the plan's "dice ... brunoise" ladder is fine if worded that way.
- Batonnet 1/4 x 1/4 x 2 in: SUPPORTED.
- Julienne 1/8 x 1/8 x 2 in: SUPPORTED.
- Fine julienne 1/16 in: SUPPORTED. "Fine Julienne – 1/16" x 1/16" x 2""
- Uniform cuts cook evenly: SUPPORTED. "Uniform cuts promote even cooking and are appealing to the eye."
- Slice dimension: NOT FOUND. The page gives none. Any "about as thick as a coin" is the lesson's own suggestion.
- Not on the page (lesson's own): "dice is made from sticks" steps, a slice/dice/julienne definition in words (only sizes were confirmed), the "batonnet is cut into small dice" claim (Wikipedia says batonnet "is the starting point for the small dice").

## 4. Wikipedia, "List of culinary knife cuts"
Address: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_culinary_knife_cuts (opened)
Cite: YES, as a reference list, for size ranges. Not for the plan's naming claim.

- Julienne: PARTLY, and different from Rouxbe. "approximately 1/8 by 1/8 by 1–2 inches"; also called allumette (matchstick) "when used on potatoes."
- Batonnet: "approximately 1/4 by 1/4 by 2–2+1/2 inches"; "the starting point for the small dice."
- "Naming varies; julienne, batonnet and baton are one cut at different thicknesses": NOT SUPPORTED. The page has no "baton" entry and does not compare naming across sources. Leave this claim out. What is supported: sizes vary a little between sources (julienne length 1 to 2 in here, 2 in Rouxbe, 2 1/2 in Escoffier), and julienne on potatoes is called allumette.
- Most entries cite The Professional Chef (CIA), per the page text read.
