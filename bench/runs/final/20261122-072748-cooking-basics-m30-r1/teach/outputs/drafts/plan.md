# Lesson plan: O2

Objective O2: Choose the right knife and cut for a job: chef's, paring or serrated knife, and slice, dice or julienne.
Length: 30 minutes, 4,500 words (150 per minute; floor 3,600).
Meta tags for the lesson: p3-minutes 30, p3-objective O2.

## Title

**Knife and cut selection: matching the blade and the cut size to the job**

## The win

Anyone who learns this can look at a food and a job, name the knife whose blade fits it, and name the cut (slice, dice or julienne) that gives pieces of the right shape and size.

## What the learner brings (from the files)

- Knows the pinch grip and claw grip (learning record 0001). Link back to that lesson; use it as the warm-up recall.
- Knows which drawer knife is big and which is small, but not which job suits which, and not what slice, dice or julienne mean (NOTES.md).
- Mission: cooks at home most days, keeps cutting themselves or struggling. Every example is a home-cooking cut: onion, carrot, tomato, bread, apple, garlic, potato, chicken.
- Preferences: example first, then the idea; real numbers; casual voice; short paragraphs; feedback that points at the exact wrong part; a "why this matters in the real world" line at the end.
- Note: NOTES.md says the learner thinks a dull knife is safer. That belongs to the sharpness lesson (O3), so here it appears only as a short pointer in Part 1, not a taught topic.

## Warm-up (inside Part 1, counted in its budget)

Two recall questions, answers on demand: (1) Which hand pinches the blade, and which curls the fingertips? (2) What do the knuckles do in the claw grip? Both come from learning record 0001.

## Part 1: Task fit: why the job picks the knife

**Key sentence:** A knife slips when it is the wrong size or shape for the food, so before you cut you ask what the job is. This comes first because the rest of the lesson is just answers to that one question.

**Word budget:** 500 (warm-up 120, example 200, idea and misconception 130, practice check 50). Extra detail goes into a numbered trace of the example.

**Worked example (first, then the idea):** Trace of a home cook slicing a ripe tomato with a small knife from the drawer.
1. The tomato has a slippery skin and a soft middle.
2. The blade edge skids on the skin.
3. The cook pushes harder.
4. The blade breaks through suddenly and the force carries into the holding hand.
5. Result: a cut on a fingertip, and a squashed tomato.
Then the same tomato with a serrated knife (previewed only; the full explanation is in Part 3): the teeth bite the skin and no hard push is needed. The lesson's idea: a good match means less force, and less force means fewer slips.

**Figures:** none. A three-row table in Part 2 carries the comparison.

**Misconceptions:**
- "A small knife is the safe knife for everything." Real case: Escoffier says never force a paring knife; if you are straining, switch to a heavier blade ([Escoffier](https://escoffier.edu/blog/culinary-arts/different-knives-and-the-best-uses-for-each)). A paring knife on a raw carrot or squash is the textbook slip.
- Pointer only: "a dull knife is safer" is covered in the sharpening lesson; say in one line that dull blades need more force and raise injury risk (same Escoffier source), and that the next lesson shows why.

## Part 2: Chef's knife: the all-purpose blade for big jobs

**Key sentence:** One knife covers most of what you do, so start by learning it well. It comes second because the other two knives are exceptions to it.

**Word budget:** 800 (basic explanation 250, example trace 300, table 100, practice with feedback 150). Extra detail: a second concrete food (a carrot, after the onion) showing the same idea, no new topic.

**Worked example:** Cutting an onion for soup with a chef's knife, as a trace.
1. The onion is wide, about 3 inches across, and firm.
2. The 8-inch blade is long enough to cut through the whole onion in one pass.
3. The wide blade gives the knuckles of the claw hand a flat side to guide against (links to the earlier grip lesson).
4. The weight of the blade does the cutting, so the hand presses little.
5. Result: a clean, controlled cut.
Facts: blade usually 8 to 10 inches, can be 6 to 14; used for slicing, dicing, julienning, onions, carrots, herbs, melons, poultry, cabbage ([Escoffier](https://escoffier.edu/blog/culinary-arts/different-knives-and-the-best-uses-for-each)). Say that a knife whose weight and balance feel comfortable lets you work at a smooth pace.

**Figures:**
- Figure 1, "Three knives, drawn to scale." Shows a chef's knife (8 in), a paring knife (3.5 in or less) and a serrated knife (label blade "long, toothed"; no length claimed for it). Boxes: "chef's: the big blade", "paring: the small blade", "serrated: the saw-edged blade". Arrows: none; just labels. Placed after the first mention of the three names in Part 2.

**Misconceptions:**
- "A bigger knife is more dangerous." Case: onion, 3 inches wide. A 3-inch blade must be pushed through, so it needs force and slips; an 8-inch blade glides through under its own weight.
- "The chef's knife is just for chefs." Case: the Escoffier list of home foods (onions, carrots, chicken, cabbage) is the learner's weekly shopping list.

## Part 3: Paring and serrated knives: the two special-purpose blades

**Key sentence:** Two jobs the chef's knife does badly, tiny detail work and slippery or crusty foods, each get their own knife. They come after the chef's knife because you pick them only when the chef's knife is the wrong fit.

**Word budget:** 900 (paring 250, serrated 250, one example trace 250, table recap and practice 150). Extra detail: a second food for each knife (garlic and strawberry for paring; bread for serrated).

**Worked example:** One tomato, two knives, side by side (the same tomato as in Part 1, so it closes the loop).
Paring knife: core the stem end (small, in the hand, close to the fingertips; blade no longer than 3.5 in).
Serrated knife: slice the tomato in rounds with a gentle sawing motion, teeth bite the skin, inside is not crushed.
Chef's knife (if sharp) works for the slicing too, but a dull one squashes it. (Do not claim a dull-knife rule beyond Escoffier's statement.)
Trace for the serrated cut: 1. teeth touch skin; 2. light back-and-forth saw; 3. skin parts, no downward shove; 4. slice falls away whole.
Facts: paring knife for peeling apples and potatoes, coring tomatoes, trimming small, tender produce such as garlic, ginger, strawberries; not for hard carrots, beets or squash. Serrated knife for bread, tomatoes, oranges, strawberries; the cut is not as clean as a chef's-knife cut. Serrated blades do not go on a honing rod (flag only; the sharpening lesson covers it) ([Escoffier](https://escoffier.edu/blog/culinary-arts/different-knives-and-the-best-uses-for-each)). Rule of thumb for the serrated knife: tough or slippery outside, soft inside.

**Figures:**
- Figure 2, a table (not an SVG): rows are the three knives; columns are "Best for", "Not for", "Home example". Chef's: onion, carrot, chicken / tiny peeling; paring: garlic, coring a tomato, peeling an apple / hard carrot or squash; serrated: bread, tomato, orange / chopping an onion. Placed at the end of Part 3, after all three knives are explained.

**Misconceptions:**
- "A serrated knife is only for bread." Case: tomato and orange (Escoffier).
- "The paring knife can handle a carrot because the carrot is small." Case: a 5-inch carrot is small but hard; Escoffier says the paring knife is not for carrots, beets or squash.
- "Serrated knives get honed like the others." Flag only; fully handled in O3.

## Part 4: Slice and dice: cutting into flat pieces or into cubes

**Key sentence:** Once you have the knife, the next question is what shape and size you want the food in, and the two most common answers are flat pieces or small cubes. This comes before julienne because dice is built from sticks, which Part 5 shows.

**Word budget:** 800 (slice 200, dice 300, worked trace 200, practice 100). Extra detail: the full dice size ladder and one more food (potato) as a second case.

**Worked example:** A potato for roast potatoes, as a numbered trace.
1. Goal: pieces that finish cooking at the same time.
2. Decision: dice, not slice; choose medium dice, 1/2-inch cubes ([Rouxbe](https://rouxbe.com/tips-techniques/495-types-of-knife-cuts)).
3. Cut the potato into 1/2-inch slabs (a slice).
4. Stack the slabs and cut 1/2-inch sticks.
5. Turn the sticks and cut across every 1/2 inch.
6. Result: 1/2-inch cubes; a 3/4-inch cube would take longer to cook than a 1/4-inch one.
Definitions, each given where it first appears: slice = a flat piece cut across the food, no fixed size (the Rouxbe page gives no dimension; say so and give a home guide like "about as thick as a coin" only as the lesson's own suggestion, marked as such). Dice = a cube: large 3/4 in, medium 1/2 in, small 1/4 in, brunoise 1/8 in. The reason: uniform pieces cook evenly and look better ("Uniform cuts promote even cooking", Rouxbe).

**Figures:**
- Figure 3, "From slab to stick to cube." Three steps drawn left to right: box "slab, 1/2 in thick" arrow "cut lengthwise" to box "stick, 1/2 in square" arrow "cut across every 1/2 in" to box "cube, 1/2 in all sides". Placed after the dice definition and before the trace. Plot only those numbers.

**Misconceptions:**
- "Dice means chop it any way, small." Case: if one carrot piece is 1/4 in and another 3/4 in, in a pot of 10 minutes the small ones are mush and the big ones raw in the middle.
- "Slice and dice are the same thing." Case: a sliced tomato fits a sandwich; a diced one goes in salsa.

## Part 5: Julienne: cutting into thin matchsticks

**Key sentence:** When you want thin even strips instead of cubes, you cut julienne, which is the same slab-then-stick steps as dice with thinner pieces. It comes last of the three cuts because it is the thinnest and needs the steadiest hands.

**Word budget:** 800 (definition and size ladder 250, trace 250, safety link to the grips 150, practice 150). Extra detail: a second food (bell pepper) and one more line on fine julienne.

**Worked example:** A carrot for a stir-fry or slaw, as a numbered trace.
1. Goal: thin sticks that cook in under two minutes in a hot pan.
2. Decision: julienne, 1/8 in square and about 2 in long ([Rouxbe](https://rouxbe.com/tips-techniques/495-types-of-knife-cuts)); Escoffier teaches 2 1/2 in ([Escoffier](https://escoffier.edu/blog/culinary-arts/different-knives-and-the-best-uses-for-each) is not the source for this; use the Escoffier culinary-school search result at https://www.escoffier.edu/?p=23845 only if re-checked while writing). Say sources differ slightly: about 2 to 2 1/2 in.
3. Trim the carrot to 2 in lengths.
4. Cut one flat side so the carrot sits still (stability: this is the real safety step; a round carrot rolls).
5. Slice 1/8 in planks, stack them, cut 1/8 in sticks.
6. Use the claw grip, knuckles guiding the blade.
7. Result: a pile of matchsticks. Cutting those across gives brunoise, 1/8 in cubes.
Terms: batonnet = 1/4 in square, 2 in long (thicker, cut into small dice); julienne = 1/8 in; fine julienne = 1/16 in.

**Figures:**
- Figure 4, "The stick ladder." Boxes, top to bottom: "batonnet, 1/4 in square" arrow "cut thinner" to "julienne, 1/8 in square" arrow "cut across" to "brunoise, 1/8 in cube". Plot only these numbers. Placed after the julienne definition.

**Misconceptions:**
- "Julienne is any thin strip." Case: a 1/2-inch strip of pepper is a baton, not julienne; julienne is 1/8 in, 4 times thinner.
- "Cutting a round carrot as-is is fine." Case: a round carrot rolls under the blade; flatten one side first.
- Naming varies: some references treat julienne, batonnet and baton as one cut at different thicknesses (reported by search results; check the Wikipedia list of culinary knife cuts if the writer wants to note this). The lesson uses the Rouxbe numbers.

## Part 6: Putting it together: choosing the knife and the cut

**Key sentence:** Now you do the whole choice yourself: first the job, then the knife, then the cut. It comes last because it uses every earlier part together.

**Word budget:** 700 for the worked example and practice, plus 300 for summary, prompt and go-further = 1,000. Extra detail: a second, fully faded practice case.

**Worked example (full):** Dinner: stir-fry with chicken, carrot, garlic, and a crusty loaf on the side.
1. Chicken breast into 1-in cubes: chef's knife, dice.
2. Carrot: chef's knife, julienne.
3. Garlic: paring knife (small, tender), small dice.
4. Bread: serrated knife, slices.
Each line has its reason, in a short trace.

**Practice (faded steps):**
- Task 1, steps shown with blanks: "Salsa: tomato, onion, jalapeno." Learner fills in knife and cut for each; hints first (what is the outside like? how big is the piece?); solution on demand.
- Task 2, steps fully faded: "Apple slaw: an apple and a cabbage, plus a bread roll to serve." Learner writes knife, cut and one reason for each; hint, then solution.
Practice questions must not be reused in the quiz; the quiz uses fresh foods (not salsa, slaw, tomato core, stir-fry).

**Summary (three to five lines):**
1. Pick the job first, the knife second, the cut third.
2. Chef's knife for big, firm foods; paring for small, tender detail work; serrated for tough or slippery outside, soft inside.
3. Slice is a flat piece; dice is a cube (1/2 in is medium); julienne is a thin stick (1/8 in square).
4. Uniform pieces cook evenly.
5. Prompt: "Explain to a friend why you would not use a paring knife on a carrot."

**Why it matters (real world):** one line: the right knife needs less force, which is the biggest single reason slips happen at home.

**Go further and closing:** primary source link; two to three other sources; the community suggestion (r/Cooking or r/chefknives on Reddit for testing; if used, the writer must find and verify a URL first, since none was seen this session); reminder to ask follow-ups in chat and that a short quiz is ready; a pointer that the next lesson covers sharpening, honing and storing the knife.
